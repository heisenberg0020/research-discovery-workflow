# Q0–Q2: discover a field and form opportunities

## Q0 — Neutral research contract

**Entry:** New exploration is requested, and the actual isolation boundary is established.

1. State the scientific domain and scope, user's interests, desired kind of contribution, negotiable boundaries, actual search cutoff, and output root. Do not inherit a dataset, architecture, technology, hardware budget or requirement to beat a benchmark from previous work.
2. Distinguish scientific goal from a preferred implementation. Optional lenses are A: source of benefit and cost; B: important phenomenon and its explanation; C: better treatment of a concrete bottleneck. They may overlap; do not impose one candidate per lens or limit other valid research contributions.
3. Clarify only unknowns that materially change the task. Unknown resources can become conditional branches. Establish whether the user wants a single pass, two passes, personal investment prioritization, and any narrower stop point. If unspecified, complete one pass through Q7; offer the two-pass mode rather than automatically doubling work.
4. State allowed evidence sources and any actual experiment scope. Ordinary public research and static code understanding are part of discovery. Model/data evaluation, simulation, training or user studies are experiments even if small; do not silently launch them as “reading.”

**Exit:** A usable neutral contract, remaining unknowns and authorized scope in `RUN.md`/brief. No preselected winning candidate.

## Q1-T — Domain-down: what matters in this field?

**Entry:** The neutral contract. Do not receive the method-up route's candidate shortlist first.

1. Map activities, stakeholder/scientific goals, operating settings, decisions, and constraints. Use current primary papers, conference/program signals, surveys as navigation, practitioner/industry reports, standards, and public discussions as appropriate to the field. Forums and attention signals help discover questions; follow them to evidence rather than treating popularity as proof.
2. Convert promising signals into a conditional account: **setting → desired outcome → present treatment → observed limitation or dispute → consequence**. Ask what is already working. Distinguish historic difficulty from the problem's current form; follow directly relevant later treatments.
3. For industry evidence, identify what is actually reported: deployed use, online test, shadow operation, retrospective analysis, or offline benchmark. Company prominence, deployment alone, and confidential missing details do not prove field-wide importance or an unsolved opportunity. For non-industrial fields, use the corresponding scientific/clinical/social consequences instead of forcing an enterprise story.
4. Look for contrary evidence and missing viewpoints that could materially change the account. State visibility bias and coverage limits. A weak but grounded signal may remain a hypothesis; neither an unmeasured effect nor absence of a paper proves a problem.
5. Save the first-pass domain judgments and sources before seeing U's candidate judgments.

**Exit:** Problem portraits with current answers, conditions, consequences, disputes and blind spots. Partial coverage is labeled, not hidden.

## Q1-U — Method-up: what works, and what could change?

**Entry:** The same neutral contract; T does not decide U's allowable method list.

1. Independently explore method families, effective principles, theoretical conditions, differing results, and simpler alternatives. Search successful explanations as well as failures. Do not start from a fixed technology taxonomy or only list generic limitations.
2. Reconstruct important methods: available inputs → variables/representation → operations or reasoning → objective/signal → output/decision. Read the original method and applicable proof/experiment/implementation details, not just abstracts.
3. Trace how mechanism differences could change knowledge, capability, or a decision. A successful existing method may inspire a new operation; a failure is not required. Distinguish form/name differences from meaningful operational differences.
4. Save the first-pass mechanism/opportunity judgments and unresolved questions before exchange.

**Exit:** Working mechanisms and plausible consequence-linked opportunities, or explicit understanding gaps. No corresponding T portrait is required to keep a credible U opportunity.

## Q1-S — Compare the two reasoning routes

**Entry:** T/U first-pass records, or explicit partial-route status. Two sections written after sharing all judgments are not unexposed first passes.

1. Align conditional claims on task, goal, available information, constraints, operation, timing and evaluation. Classify actual support, conflict, scope mismatch, one-route opportunity, sufficient treatment, and unknown. Do not pair every item or select only the intersection.
2. Write the bridge: **which goal or phenomenon connects to which mechanism, why, and with what consequence?** Mark facts, inference and hypotheses. “Worth further research” is not a bridge.
3. Resolve consequential ambiguity with targeted rereading. Check the current strongest treatment and its assumptions. Distinguish decisive readable material, reading that can proceed alongside ideation, and unknowns requiring new scientific evidence. Narrow an unverified judgment instead of blocking every opportunity.
4. Compare the new understanding against the actual first-pass records: unchanged, deepened, newly connected, substantively revised, or still disputed. Do not manufacture a synthesis discovery. Keep meaningful single-route ideas and explain unpaired branches.
5. Deliver a standalone-readable stage analysis: field understanding and coverage; relations/omissions between routes; real changes; concrete onward questions. A question cluster can remain broad, but a handoff object should connect its condition, existing treatment, desired understanding/change, consequence, and key uncertainty. Full new mechanisms belong in Q2–Q3, not as an entry gate here.

**Exit:** A useful comparison and onward objects, with honest missing-route status. This stage does not authorize earlier exposure to previous research results.

## Q2 — Turn understanding into concrete sketches

**Entry:** Evidence supports discussing an object and consequence, even with explicit assumptions or partial synthesis.

1. Form genuinely different questions or operations; no fixed candidate quota. Develop a few promising objects deeply enough to compare scientific substance. Avoid splitting names to manufacture diversity.
2. Keep three layers distinct: **L1 broader importance; L2 specific remaining opportunity and its consequence; L3 our proposed explanation/operation**. A fourth, personal investment priority, is used only if requested; it must not prevent forming a proposal.
3. Connect **conditions → remaining room in a current mechanism/explanation → observable outcome/decision → meaningful consequence**. Unknown prevalence or effect size stays unknown, not zero or invented. A novel explanatory insight or better trade-off can be valuable even without a production-loss story.
4. Sketch different mechanisms where justified, or one new mechanism with a serious simple/existing alternative. Do not require a novel question, failed old method, certified novelty or pilot success before attempting a concrete design.
5. Target source/code/data-documentation reading to judgments, not repository stars or ease of reuse. Keep hypotheses visible and continue substantive construction rather than writing “find a mechanism later.”

**Exit:** Opportunity sketches with object, mechanism basis, consequence, proposed relation/operation and evidence identity. Rough question clusters are not yet mature proposals. Read the evidence/mechanism instructions before Q3.
